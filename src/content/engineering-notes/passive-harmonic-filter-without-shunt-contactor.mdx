This is an operating condition that deserves attention during the design and selection of passive harmonic filters.

Passive harmonic filters (PHFs) are commonly installed on the input side of variable frequency drives (VFDs) to reduce harmonic currents and improve overall power quality.

Many PHF designs use a combination of series inductors and a tuned reactor-capacitor network. These components work together to reduce harmonic distortion under the intended operating conditions.

However, the filter's capacitive reactive power does not necessarily decrease in proportion to the VFD's load.

At very light loading, or when the drive is stopped while the filter remains energized, this characteristic can become important.

In some systems, the result may be a leading power factor and an increase in voltage at the common electrical bus.

The consequences depend on the filter design and the electrical characteristics of the installation.

For a broader look at changing filter operating points, see [Passive Harmonic Filters at Partial Load](/engineering-notes/passive-harmonic-filters-partial-load/).

## 1. What Happens at Normal VFD Loading?

When the VFD operates within its intended loading range, the passive harmonic filter reduces harmonic currents associated with the drive's rectifier.

The reactor-capacitor network also affects the fundamental-frequency reactive power balance.

Depending on the filter design and the characteristics of the connected drive, this may improve the overall power factor.

At normal loading, the filter's reactive power contribution may be appropriately balanced by the electrical system.

However, this operating condition does not represent every possible loading scenario.

A VFD that normally operates at high loading may spend considerable time running at reduced output, particularly in variable-torque applications such as pumps and fans.

## 2. What Changes When the VFD Load Decreases?

As VFD loading decreases, the real power drawn by the drive generally decreases.

The reactive power demand associated with the drive and the overall loading conditions can also change.

However, if the passive filter's capacitor branch remains connected, it continues to exchange capacitive reactive power with the AC system.

For a fixed capacitor operating at a given frequency, its reactive power is strongly influenced by the applied voltage and capacitance rather than being directly controlled by the motor's load percentage.

The complete behavior of a tuned harmonic filter also depends on the associated inductors and the filter's design.

Consequently, the filter may provide more capacitive reactive power than the connected loads require at a particular operating point.

The net system power factor may then become leading.

This is not necessarily a fault. Whether it creates an unacceptable condition depends on the wider electrical system.

## 3. What Happens When the VFD Is OFF?

Now consider a VFD that has stopped operating while its input supply and passive harmonic filter remain energized.

The motor is no longer producing mechanical output.

However, unless the filter's capacitor branch is disconnected through an appropriate switching arrangement, that branch may remain connected to the AC supply.

In this condition, the harmonic-producing load may be very small or absent, but the filter can still contribute capacitive reactive power.

Depending on the network conditions, this reactive power may contribute to a voltage rise at the common bus.

This is one reason light-load behavior must be considered when selecting a passive harmonic filter.

<Callout type="important-note" title="A stopped VFD is not necessarily isolated">
A stopped VFD is not necessarily electrically isolated. The behavior described here assumes that the upstream supply remains energized and the capacitor branch remains connected. An energized drive may still draw a small amount of standby or internal-loss current even when no motor-load current is shown in the illustration.
</Callout>

## 4. Why Can the Common-Bus Voltage Rise?

The relationship between reactive power and voltage depends on the electrical impedance between the supply and the point of connection.

A capacitor branch can provide leading reactive power to the electrical system.

If the source has appreciable impedance, this reactive power contribution can affect the voltage at the common bus.

The magnitude of the resulting voltage change depends on several factors:

- The equivalent source impedance and available short-circuit strength.
- The filter's effective capacitive reactive power.
- The loading and reactive power requirements of other connected equipment.
- The operating voltage and characteristics of the filter.
- The location of the filter within the electrical distribution system.
- The presence of other capacitor banks, harmonic filters, or resonance conditions.

A relatively stiff electrical supply may experience only a small voltage change.

A weaker supply with greater effective impedance may be more sensitive to capacitive reactive power.

The voltage change caused by decreasing load should also be distinguished from the additional voltage effect associated with the energized filter capacitor branch.

Both can influence the observed operating voltage.

The purpose of the example is to demonstrate why a capacitor branch that remains energized at very low load deserves engineering attention.

## 6. What About Other Loads Connected to the Same Bus?

This is an important system-level consideration.

A passive harmonic filter is commonly selected based on the characteristics of one VFD.

However, that VFD may share an electrical distribution bus with other equipment.

Examples include:

- Additional VFDs and motor controllers.
- Control power transformers and associated equipment.
- Electronic power supplies.
- Lighting systems.
- Other voltage-sensitive industrial equipment.

If the filter's operating condition produces a significant common-bus voltage rise, other equipment connected to that same bus may experience the higher voltage.

Depending on the magnitude and duration of the voltage increase, possible consequences include nuisance protection trips, increased electrical stress, or operation outside an equipment manufacturer's specified voltage range.

These effects are not inevitable.

The actual risk must be evaluated against equipment voltage ratings, protection settings, source characteristics, and measured or calculated system conditions.

The key point is that the filter's influence may extend beyond the VFD it was selected to serve.

## 7. What Is the Role of a Shunt Contactor?

In this application, the term *shunt contactor* refers to a contactor used to switch the capacitor-containing shunt branch of a passive harmonic filter.

It is more precisely described as a capacitor-branch switching contactor.

Its purpose is to disconnect the capacitor branch when that branch is not required, or when its reactive power contribution would be undesirable.

For example, a suitable manufacturer-approved control arrangement may disconnect the capacitor branch when the associated VFD is stopped or operating below a defined load condition.

When the VFD returns to an operating condition that requires harmonic filtering, the capacitor branch may be reconnected according to the manufacturer's recommended sequence.

This can help avoid an unnecessarily leading power factor and reduce the potential for voltage rise under particular operating conditions.

However, switching the capacitor branch out also removes the tuned LC filter's intended harmonic mitigation during that period.

If a series input reactor remains connected, it may still provide some current smoothing or impedance, but this is not equivalent to having the complete tuned harmonic filter operating.

For this reason, capacitor switching must be treated as part of the filter's overall engineering design rather than as a universal solution.

Contactor selection, capacitor-switching duty, control logic, discharge time, switching transients, and interlocking must follow the manufacturer's instructions.

<Callout type="warning" title="Validate the complete filter design">
Do not add or bypass a capacitor-branch switching device without validating the design.
</Callout>

## 8. Does Every Passive Harmonic Filter Need a Shunt Contactor?

**No.**

This is an important distinction.

Passive harmonic filters do not all have the same capacitor kVAr ratings, filter configurations, or light-load characteristics.

Some manufacturers deliberately minimize capacitive reactive power to maintain acceptable behavior across a broad operating range.

For these designs, disconnecting the capacitor branch at light load may not be necessary.

Other filter designs or particular system conditions may benefit from a manufacturer-approved capacitor-switching option.

The decision depends on the filter and the application, not simply on whether the motor operates below an arbitrary percentage of rated load.

There is no universal rule requiring capacitor disconnection whenever a VFD falls below 20% loading.

Generator-fed systems may require additional evaluation because the generator's ability to absorb leading reactive power can be limited.

The filter manufacturer's technical guidance and the generator's reactive power capability should be reviewed where applicable.

## 9. What Should Engineers Verify Before Selecting a PHF?

Before finalizing a passive harmonic filter, consider the following questions:

1. What is the actual operating-load profile of the VFD, including extended low-load or idle periods?
2. What is the filter's effective capacitive reactive power at various operating points?
3. How does the filter perform at minimum load and when the VFD is stopped?
4. What are the available short-circuit strength and source impedance at the point of connection?
5. Could the resulting reactive power affect the common-bus voltage or other connected equipment?
6. Is the system supplied by a utility transformer or an on-site generator?
7. Does the manufacturer offer or recommend capacitor-branch switching for this model?
8. What happens to harmonic mitigation when the capacitor branch is disconnected?
9. Are switching coordination, discharge, and control requirements addressed?
10. Have the actual operating conditions been verified against manufacturer data or a suitable power-system study?

These questions help assess the filter as part of the overall electrical distribution system rather than as an isolated accessory to the drive. When evaluating a multi-drive bus, the related comparison [PHF vs AHF vs Hybrid](/engineering-notes/phf-vs-ahf-vs-hybrid/) also shows why the actual load profile matters.

## 10. Final Engineering Takeaway

A passive harmonic filter should not be evaluated only at full load.

Its performance at very light load—and particularly when the associated VFD is stopped but the filter remains energized—can be equally important.

An energized capacitor branch may contribute leading reactive power and, depending on the electrical system, cause an undesirable common-bus voltage rise.

A properly designed capacitor-branch switching arrangement can be beneficial in some applications.

In other applications, a low-kVAr filter may operate satisfactorily without switching.

<Callout type="calculation-result" title="Engineering objective">
The engineering objective is not to install a shunt contactor in every passive harmonic filter. It is to understand the filter's light-load behavior and select the appropriate design for the actual power system.
</Callout>
